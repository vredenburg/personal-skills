# Editorial rubric

Use this rubric as a decision aid, not a blacklist. Revise a feature when it is generic, recurrent, unsupported, or nonfunctional in this particular text. Preserve it when it is precise, purposeful, genre-appropriate, or characteristic of the established voice.

## Decide whether to intervene

Ask:

1. Does the wording add information, emphasis, rhythm, or characterization?
2. Is its implied fact, feeling, or authority supported by the text or authorized context?
3. Does the same construction recur often enough to feel automatic?
4. Would changing it erase a deliberate feature of this author, speaker, genre, or house style?
5. Can a smaller edit solve the problem without recasting the whole passage?

One conspicuous defect can justify an edit, but an isolated word, dash, fragment, passive clause, or three-part list does not establish a pattern.

## Diagnostic families

### Empty elevation and promotion

Watch for ceremony that inflates ordinary facts into milestones, symbols, transformations, legacies, or evidence of a sweeping trend. Promotional adjectives and claims of importance are suspect when the text never demonstrates them.

Trim ceremonial wording without adding evidence, strengthening the claim, or discarding the author's substantive evaluation.

### Abstraction and unsupported authority

Watch for abstract nouns replacing actors and actions, vague groups presented as authorities, and claims attributed to unnamed experts, observers, reports, or public opinion. Also notice weak claims padded with multiple layers of caution.

Do not manufacture a source or a firmer claim. Name an existing source only when the material establishes that attribution; otherwise preserve the vague attribution and necessary uncertainty, flagging missing support when useful.

### Canned framing and residue

Remove throat-clearing, tutorial announcements, generic introductions, repeated summaries, automatic reassurances, upbeat conclusions without substance, and chatbot courtesies accidentally embedded in the prose. A heading should not be followed by a sentence that merely repeats it.

Retain orientation that the reader genuinely needs, especially in long or technical documents.

### Mechanical rhetorical architecture

Look for repeated forms rather than banning any one form:

- serial "not X but Y" contrasts;
- ideas forced into threes, false ranges, or overly balanced pairs;
- paragraphs built from the same opener, cadence, or closing kicker;
- rhetorical questions immediately answered by the writer;
- successive labels for the same subject merely to avoid repeating its name;
- trailing participial clauses that simulate analysis without adding a clear relationship.

Keep a device when it earns emphasis, comedy, characterization, or rhythm. When it becomes a template, state the relationship directly or vary the underlying syntax for a reason.

### Redundancy and over-explanation

Cut sentences that translate, announce, or summarize a point the reader has just understood. Remove excessive setup around a simple claim. Preserve repetition that develops an argument, controls pacing, or carries emotional and rhetorical force.

### Imported stance or personality

Generic enthusiasm, reassurance, false intimacy, forced balance, and unearned emotional color can make prose sound assembled. Do not cure neutrality by adding first person, jokes, tangents, strong opinions, mixed feelings, or confessional detail. Voice must come from the author and the text, not from a stock performance of personality.

### Diction, imagery, and cadence

Watch for clusters of fashionable abstractions, needlessly elaborate substitutes for simple verbs, strained or mixed metaphors, explained metaphors, uniform sentence lengths, and a polished punch line at the end of every paragraph.

Prefer the most exact phrasing already supported by the material. Do not mechanically shorten every sentence, alternate lengths, replace every form of "is," or roughen grammar to manufacture irregularity.

### Formatting and grammar are contextual

Passive voice, em dashes, fragments, sentence-initial conjunctions, rhetorical questions, bold labels, lists, title capitalization, emoji, smart quotation marks, and compound hyphens can all be correct. Preserve them when they serve grammar, readability, genre, typography, or established voice. Edit their overuse, not their existence.

## Fiction checks

Build a compact voice fingerprint before revising: POV and tense, narrative distance, sentence movement, diction, figurative density, dialogue habits, paragraph rhythm, and the kinds of detail the narrator notices.

Then check that the edit:

- does not give a narrator or character knowledge they lacked;
- does not invent motivation, sensation, staging, world detail, or thematic explanation;
- does not standardize distinct dialogue or interior voices;
- does not resolve ambiguity, subtext, or silence that the scene relies on;
- does not accelerate or flatten a passage whose repetition controls tension;
- remains consistent with supplied or explicitly in-scope canon.

Example: preserve a purposeful device.

> The bell rang. Once. Twice. It was not merely the signal for curfew; it was a reminder that the gates were already locked.

Leave unchanged. The bell both signals curfew and serves as a reminder that the gates are already locked. The contrast foregrounds that second function, and the fragments carry tension. Any stylistic recast should preserve these effects as well as the stated events.

## Nonfiction checks

Make a mental ledger of claims, sources, qualifiers, defined terms, names, numbers, and commitments before revising. Check the ledger afterward.

Preserve legal, scientific, and technical qualifications even when they slow the prose. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or intentionally de-emphasized. Keep document structure and terminology when readers depend on them for navigation or precision.

Example:

> Before: It is worth noting that some observers say the trial may have improved retention. The company reported that 68% of participants remained after six months.
>
> After: Some observers say the trial may have improved retention. The company reported that 68% of participants remained after six months.

The revision removes introductory padding while retaining the observers' interpretation, its uncertainty, and the company's attributed figure. If a source note is useful, flag the unnamed observers separately. The percentage alone does not establish improvement.

## Minimal-edit examples

Inflated phrasing:

> Before: In 2008, the community carried out the opening of the archive with the aim of preserving its history.
>
> After: In 2008, the community opened the archive to preserve its history.

The source supplies both the actor and the purpose. The revision simplifies the wording without inferring either.

Redundant explanation for readers familiar with file permissions:

> Before: The export failed because the destination was read-only. In other words, the system could not write the file there.
>
> After: The export failed because the destination was read-only.

For readers unfamiliar with "read-only," keep or integrate the explanation.

Repeated sentence openings in a routine status update:

> Before: Maya checked the figures. Maya found a duplicate entry. Maya removed the duplicate entry. Maya sent the corrected report.
>
> After: After checking the figures and finding a duplicate entry, Maya removed that entry. Then Maya sent the corrected report.

The actions and their order remain intact. The sentence rhythm changes without shortening the passage.

These examples illustrate decisions, not preferred sentence shapes. Match the live text rather than imitating them.
