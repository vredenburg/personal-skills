# Editing fiction

Use the requested editing strength and the passage's established voice to judge how much to change. The examples below illustrate local decisions; they do not prescribe a style for every story.

## Let voice guide the revision

Build a compact voice fingerprint from the passage and any supplied sample: POV and tense, narrative distance, sentence movement, diction, figurative density, dialogue habits, paragraph rhythm, and the kinds of detail the narrator notices. Connect those observations to the choices being made. A narrator who delays a sentence's main point may need a cleaner route to that ending; an evasive speaker may need a less cumbersome line that remains just as evasive.

Use supplied examples of accepted or rejected edits, and the author's reasons, to choose between otherwise suitable revisions for this voice or project. Apply the current brief first. Do not turn a preference for one passage into a universal rule or import facts from a voice sample.

Identify the specific weakness an edit should resolve and consider the surrounding paragraph before choosing a replacement. Recast a tangled sentence while retaining its movement; remove scaffolding around an existing image; simplify an awkward utterance using the speaker's established diction. An intensive line edit may require substantial changes to syntax within the agreed scope. The number of changed words does not determine whether the intervention is proportionate.

Treat explicit thoughts, feelings, and interpretations as content. An action that could suggest an emotion does not make a sentence naming that emotion redundant. When explanation is doing separate narrative work, improve its expression without silently deleting it.

## Worked decisions

### Untangle a flowing sentence

Context: the first-person narrator's recollections often unfold in long sentences, with a decision arriving near the end.

> Before: I had intended, when I set out that morning, to take the short road by the mill, but the thought of reaching the house before anyone else had arrived was what made me choose instead the road that went along the river.
>
> After: When I set out that morning, I intended to take the short road by the mill, but the thought of reaching the house before anyone else made me choose the river road instead.

The revision removes the cumbersome construction around the choice while retaining the long sentence, the turn from intention to decision, and the thought that prompted it. It does not supply a reason for that thought or split the recollection into a series of brisk statements.

### Distinguish an echo from a beat

In straightforward action narration, when the second sentence merely repeats the completed action:

> Before: Rae pushed the bolt across the door. The door was now bolted.
>
> After: Rae pushed the bolt across the door.

Compare a passage that pauses to register the result:

> Rae pushed the bolt across the door. Bolted. At last.

Leave this version unchanged when that pause and reaction belong to the established voice. The repeated information carries a different effect. A thought about whether the bolt will hold would also be distinct content, even if it followed the same action.

### Keep speakers distinct while recasting a line

Context: Mina habitually asks direct questions in clipped language. Her question here is straightforward, with no earlier misunderstanding to correct. Orr speaks formally.

> Before: "The thing I'm asking is whether you told her," Mina said.
>
> "I informed her that the meeting had been postponed," Orr said.
>
> After: "Did you tell her?" Mina said.
>
> "I informed her that the meeting had been postponed," Orr said.

Mina's question becomes direct while Orr's diction remains formal. Both retain their information. If the longer framing expressed insistence or corrected a misunderstanding in the live scene, the revision would need to preserve that function too.

### Bring an existing image into focus

> Before: Across the ceiling, the light from the passing cars moved in a way that made it look like pale fish swimming there.
>
> After: Across the ceiling, light from the passing cars swam like pale fish.

The revision removes the explanation surrounding the comparison. The light, movement, location, and fish image all come from the source. It adds no weather, colour, sensation, or motive and does not replace the author's image with a more elaborate one.

### Preserve a purposeful device

> The bell rang. Once. Twice. It was not merely the signal for curfew; it was a reminder that the gates were already locked.

Leave unchanged. The bell both signals curfew and serves as a reminder that the gates are already locked. The contrast foregrounds that second function, and the fragments carry tension. Any stylistic recast should preserve these effects as well as the stated events.

## Compare the passage, not only the changed sentence

Reread the revision alongside the original in paragraph context. Check that the identified weakness has improved and that the resulting sentence joins naturally to what precedes and follows it. Check timing, emphasis, and recognizable voice as well as grammatical fluency. Keep the original where an edit offers no clear benefit; revisit an unresolved weakness when the requested scope permits repairing it. Leaving effective prose alone and repairing awkward prose are both successful outcomes.

Then check that the edit:

- does not give a narrator or character knowledge they lacked;
- does not invent motivation, sensation, staging, world detail, or thematic explanation;
- does not standardize distinct dialogue or interior voices;
- does not resolve ambiguity, subtext, or silence that the scene relies on;
- does not accelerate or flatten a passage whose repetition controls tension;
- remains consistent with supplied or explicitly in-scope canon.
