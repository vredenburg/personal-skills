On Mara's last night, nine people bought tickets. Ed wrote the number in the ledger and drew a line beneath it with a ruler he kept for that purpose. There were quicker ways of keeping records, he said, but none of them made the figures any better. Mara watched the ink sink into the paper. Six years she had worked here, and she still liked that part, the moment before a mark stopped shining and became something you could touch.

After the nine had left, she went into the auditorium. Ed was in the office below the stairs, counting the money. He had told her to leave her keys on the lobby counter when she finished, and she had said yes, of course, as though there were several other arrangements she had considered and rejected. The cleaning lights were already on. In their flat brightness the screen looked like a large piece of wall someone had forgotten to paint.

Row by row. That was how June had taught her, on her first evening, before June left to look after her husband. Start at the front and don't give your eyes too much to do. Mara folded a newspaper, lifted a cup that was still warm, and found a chocolate wrapper caught in the hinge of a seat. She put the paper in her sack. She put the cup in her sack. She found that the wrapper required a little more attention in order to get it free. At the back of the room the projector window was dark.

An umbrella lay beneath the last seat of the fourth row. Its blue fabric had come loose from one rib, and a white label on the wooden handle said PATEL in faded capitals. Mara stood it against the seat at the aisle. Outside, rain tapped the high window above the exit; inside, a drop from the umbrella settled on the carpet without making a sound. She had not seen who left it. She tried to remember the nine faces and got as far as the man who had asked whether the film had subtitles.

Row by row. She moved up the slope, touching the seat backs as she passed. There was a tear in the upholstery of H12 that she had meant to mention to Ed. There was a loose screw in the end of H13 that she had also intended to tell him about. She tried the screw with her nail. It turned a fraction, then stopped. She could still tell him. This occurred to her with the unnecessary force of a discovery.

Ed appeared in the rear doorway. "What time's your bus?"

"There's another one."

"Take that umbrella down when you're done."

He had seen it from the doorway. She looked back at its bent rib, then at the floor she had just checked. She had been under the impression that she had finished this section, and the requirement to go back through it was mildly irritating. Ed disappeared towards the office. She finished the last row, went down for the umbrella, and carried it out with her sack held away from her skirt.

In the lobby she emptied the sack into the bin and put the umbrella in the stand beside two black ones. She told Ed about the seats when he came up. He wrote their numbers on the back of an envelope. Her keys made a small, ordinary noise on the counter. She had expected a different sort of noise, although she could not have said what sort would have been appropriate. Ed slid them towards the ledger. Tomorrow somebody would need them to open the doors.

Through the glass she saw her bus turn the corner, its windows lit and almost empty. "That's me," she said. Ed lifted his hand, still holding the envelope. He began switching off the auditorium lights. From where she stood, Mara could see the empty seats through the open doors. Row by row, they disappeared. She pulled up her hood and went out.
