The last ferry had gone, taking with it the two cyclists who had spent the wait explaining the river to each other, and I had the landing to myself. I had told my sister to meet me there, although she was coming by road and the house was easier to find. This had seemed, when I said it, like a practical arrangement.

I sat on the bollard where we used to split a bag of chips, keeping my coat between me and the iron, which was cold in a way that made its coldness felt through the coat anyway. Across the water, someone was shutting the café. Each chair went upside down on a table with the care you give a thing you are tired of handling. I found myself experiencing a small amount of envy at the straightforward nature of the job.

My sister had said she would be late. She had always been good at giving bad news plenty of room to arrive in, and I had brought a book to make it clear, if only to myself, that waiting had been my intention. But the light was going, and I kept reading the same line about a man entering a garden. I wanted him to get through the gate before the headlights came round the bend.
